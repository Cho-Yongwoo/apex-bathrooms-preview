What waterproofing actually does

Waterproofing is the most effective way to prevent water from penetrating a surface. It keeps moisture out of the substrate, stops leakage reaching the structure behind the tiles, and holds off the mould growth and rot that follow. It is the part of a bathroom you never see and the part that decides how long the rest of it lasts.

Done properly it keeps the substrate sound, adds years to the concrete and the tiling laid over it, cuts what you spend on repairs, and protects the value of the house. Done badly, or skipped, it is the single most expensive failure in a bathroom - because fixing it means taking the tiles off again.

We waterproof walls, floors and ceilings in bathrooms, laundries, kitchens, balconies, decks, roofs and basements. Apex Bathrooms ACT has been doing this work since 2008.

When waterproofing is required

There are three situations, and it is worth knowing which one you are in.

When a wet area is being built or renovated. Every new or renovated bathroom, ensuite and laundry has to be waterproofed before it is tiled. This is not optional and it is not a matter of taste - it is a requirement of the National Construction Code, and the work is done to AS3740:2021.

When the existing system has failed. Peeling paint, musty odours, water stains, visible mould. Any one of those is a strong indication that the waterproofing has failed and needs attention now rather than at the next renovation. You can check all four this afternoon.

When the membrane has simply aged out. A great many homes here were built between the 1960s and the 1980s, and their original bathrooms predate the standard the work is now done to. If the room has never been touched, assume the waterproofing is at the end of its life, and get it looked at before you spend money on anything laid over the top of it.

How we do it

The sequence is fixed, because the standard fixes it.

  1. Prepare and moisture-test. The substrate is cleaned, made sound and checked for moisture, and a bond breaker goes into the junctions and around the penetrations so the membrane can move with the building instead of tearing. Anything loose or damp is dealt with before a drop of membrane goes on.
  2. Two coats, with the cure the standard requires. The membrane goes on in coats, and each one cures fully before the next. Nothing is tiled over a membrane that has not cured - that is the most expensive shortcut in the trade, and it is the one we will not take to save you a day.
  3. Inspection and certificate. A licensed waterproofer inspects the finished membrane and issues the compliance certificate. Tiling starts after that, not before.

Silicone sealing of the movement joints is a separate step later in the job, not something done on the same day as the grout. It looks like a detail; it is the joint that fails first when it is rushed.

The full sequence, coat by coat, with the heights and the cure times the standard sets, is in our guide to how a waterproofing membrane goes in and how to keep the room dry afterwards.

The compliance certificate

A licensed waterproofer must issue a compliance certificate for wet-area work, and tiling does not begin until it is in hand. It is a legal requirement under the National Construction Code, not a piece of paperwork we generate to look thorough.

You receive the certificate on completion, as part of the handover pack, along with your warranty documentation and the care instructions for the tiles, grout and silicone. Keep it. If you sell the house, it is the document that proves the room was done properly.

Why the calendar matters here

Membranes cure by drying, and cold, damp air slows that down. Through a Canberra winter the cure between coats takes longer than the minimum, so between April and September we build the extra time into the program instead of pushing the tiler on early. A job booked for September to December cures in the fastest weather of the year.

That is the honest answer to "why is the bathroom sitting there doing nothing this week". Our guide to how a renovation runs, week by week shows where the cure time sits in the schedule, and what else can stretch it.

Ventilation, and why the membrane depends on it

Waterproofing keeps water out of the structure; ventilation keeps it off the surfaces, so the membrane is never asked to do a job it was not built for. If mould keeps coming back on a ceiling that was repainted last year, the membrane is usually fine and the extraction is not. Fan sizing, the warning signs and what to do about them are in our guide to waterproofing and ventilation.

Frequently asked questions

When does a room need waterproofing?
During the construction or renovation of a wet area, and again whenever the existing membrane has failed. Peeling paint, a musty smell, water stains or visible mould all point the same way.
Can you waterproof without taking the tiles off?
No. The membrane belongs under the tiles, so a failed one means lifting the tiles in the affected area, making the substrate sound again and rebuilding from there. A coating rolled over the top of existing tiles is not a wet-area membrane. That is why a waterproofing failure costs what the tiling cost.
Do I get a compliance certificate?
Yes. A licensed waterproofer inspects the work and issues the compliance certificate, and no tiling starts until it is in hand. The certificate is part of the handover pack, with your warranty paperwork and care instructions.
Does winter change the schedule?
It does. Cold slows the cure, so between April and September we build extra cure time into the program rather than pushing the tiler on early. Membranes cure fastest in the warm, dry weather from September to December.
What warranty applies to waterproofing?
Apex Bathrooms ACT offers warranties on its work, and on waterproofing the length and the terms can vary with the job verify. We settle that in writing with you before work begins.
